The quiet craft of reading the web

By a patient reader · 9 min read · Share

Most of what we do in a browser is small and repeated: scroll a little, follow a link, go back, find the paragraph we half remember. Each one is a reach for the mouse, a hunt for a target, a click. None of them is hard. Together they are most of the work.

Keyboard browsing is not about speed for its own sake. It is about keeping your attention on the words instead of on the pointer. Link hints label everything clickable; find jumps straight to the phrase you meant.

Moving through a page

Reading is mostly moving. A single key scrolls a line, another scrolls half a screen, and two more take you to the top or the bottom. Marks let you leave a place and come straight back to it.

Headings are a map

A long article already has a table of contents: its headings. An outline of them is the fastest way to see the shape of a piece and to land exactly where you want.

The best tool is the one you stop noticing.

Paragraphs are objects

Once a page is made of paragraphs, sections and lists rather than pixels, you can act on them directly: copy this paragraph, hide that sidebar, edit a line of text in place.

Working across tabs

Tabs pile up. Closing every tab from one site, pinning a handful, or saving a set to reopen tomorrow are each one short command instead of a dozen clicks.

  • Close everything matching a pattern, with a preview first
  • Save a group of tabs into a register and bring it back later
  • Undo any close, even one from an hour ago

Keeping score

It is oddly satisfying to count the clicks you didn't make. A round of keyboard golf turns a normal afternoon of browsing into a gentle game.
